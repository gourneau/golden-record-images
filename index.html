116 photographs, recovered from eight minutes of noise

In 1977 two spacecraft left Earth carrying a gold-plated copper record. Pressed into it, alongside the music, are 116 images — not as pictures, but as sound. These were decoded from a 384 kHz digitisation of the original master tapes.

The colour images below are composited in your browser, right now, from their three separate monochrome scans. Open any one to put the decode beside the original slide, and to hear the audio it came out of.

What this decoder found

The trace is sampled, not swept

Every decoder written for this record treats each stripe as a continuous brightness sweep, chopped into equal bins. It isn't. The 1977 scan converter sampled the source picture once per television scan line and held each value — so a stripe is a staircase of 262.5 plateaus, not a ramp. Looking for that clock in the audio finds it exactly where the hardware's patent says it should be:

It also dissolves a long-standing mystery: the 2017 decoder carried a hardcoded “−12 samples on even traces” that its author called a brainless heuristic. Twelve samples is one dot. It was the scan converter's own clock showing through.

Lock to the falling edge, not the peak

The sync pulse changes width between alternate stripes — deliberately, to signal which television field a line came from. So its peak moves, but its falling edge does not.

landmarktiming wander
peak of the pulse (most decoders)±100 samples
the trough after it±7
falling edge, at zero crossing±6
Two corrections, kept on the record. An earlier version of this work claimed the stripe alternation was 60 Hz mains hum at ~70% of picture amplitude. Both halves were wrong — measuring the whole record at 25× finer resolution puts it at 60.0436 Hz, which is half the scan rate and not mains. It also claimed the frame geometry was self-contradictory; that was a bad measurement of my own. Full details are in the repository.

Honest status

This page is a gallery, not the interactive decoder. The real thing — where the picture paints stripe by stripe under your control — is still being built. What you see here was decoded offline and rendered to images; the lightbox plays each frame's audio beside its picture, but it is playing the shipped audio, not decoding it live.

The images have a distinctly embossed, bas-relief look. That is a real, known, unfixed defect: nothing in the 1977 recording chain passed a constant brightness level, so flat areas decay toward mid-grey and only edges survive. It is the same flaw the 2017 decoder's author documented and could not solve, and it remains unsolved here. Correcting it is the next piece of work, and the biggest single improvement still available.

Reference images are for labelling only. Nothing on the reference side has ever fed pixel recovery — the decoder's only input is the WAV. Where a book page has not been confidently matched to one of our images, the panel says match uncertain rather than showing a guess, and some images have no reference held at all.

Colour separation order: settled. Published decoders disagreed on whether the three scans run red-green-blue or the reverse, and nobody had shown evidence either way. Flipping the toggle above answers it by eye: under B G R the Earth's oceans turn brown and the Sinai desert turns blue-grey. Under R G B the oceans are blue, the desert is tan and the Red Sea reads correctly. R G B is right, which confirms Ron Barry's 2017 table.
