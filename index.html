116 photographs, recovered from eight minutes of noise

In 1977 two spacecraft left Earth carrying a gold-plated copper record. Pressed into it, alongside the music, are 116 images — not as pictures, but as sound. These were decoded from a 384 kHz digitisation of the original master tapes.

The colour images below are composited in your browser, right now, from their three separate monochrome scans. Open any one to put the decode beside the original slide, and to hear the audio it came out of.

What this decoder found

The trace is sampled, not swept

Every decoder written for this record treats each stripe as a continuous brightness sweep, chopped into equal bins. It isn't. The 1977 scan converter sampled the source picture once per television scan line and held each value — so a stripe is a staircase of 262.5 plateaus, not a ramp. Looking for that clock in the audio finds it exactly where the hardware's patent says it should be:

It also dissolves a long-standing mystery: the 2017 decoder carried a hardcoded “−12 samples on even traces” that its author called a brainless heuristic. Twelve samples is one dot. It was the scan converter's own clock showing through.

Lock to the falling edge, not the peak

The sync pulse changes width between alternate stripes — deliberately, to signal which television field a line came from. So its peak moves, but its falling edge does not.

The embossed look, solved

Every previously published decode of this record has the same flaw: a hammered-metal, bas-relief quality where flat areas sag toward mid-grey and only edges survive. The 2017 decoder's author documented it precisely and could not fix it. It is the single most visible difference between these images and the ones that were on the web before.

The cause is an AC-coupled recording chain — a one-pole high-pass that no analogue path of that era avoided. The fix is its exact inverse, y[n] = y[n−1] + x[n] − a·x[n−1], and two details decide whether it works. It must run in raster order — along each trace and straight across every trace boundary — because that is how time ran in the recording; a per-trace version is a different filter and a bug, and it shipped here once before it was caught. And the time constant is measured, per channel, from a scene-free part of the record's own signal: 530 samples on the left channel, 295 on the right. Nothing about the pictures is assumed.

The scan direction is an orientation test

The converter scanned every slide the same way, so trace 0 is always the same physical edge and every image paints in the same direction. That makes a 180° turn special: it is the one rotation that reverses the scan while keeping its axis. A quarter turn does not — it moves the scan to the other axis, which is exactly what a portrait slide needs.

So a 180° turn is a checkable claim that a slide was mounted upside down in 1977 — and it is checkable with no reference image at all. It is the only per-image orientation evidence the record offers, and it caught a real error here: the pulsar map was the only image carrying a 180, and the only one that played backwards. It had been turned to match a printed plate. The record's orientation is restored and the plate was rotated instead.

Fifteen published plates are printed backwards

Aligning each decode against its published reproduction needs a transform that can mirror. Once it could, fifteen plates snapped into place that had never matched: the Thai elephant went from 3 matching features to 799, the Cameroon house from 3 to 503. Bali, Guatemala, Japan, Greece, Turkey, Australia and the whistling swans are all laterally inverted in print. The record had them right.

What is not a breakthrough, stated plainly

The resolution ceiling is measured and it is not ours to move. The recording chain could have carried more than 1000 elements along a stripe; the 1977 camera resolved 138–172. We already sample 1.4 to 1.9 times finer than the camera could see, in both directions. Everything the camera captured is in these pictures, and anything sharper that a program might now produce would be invented, not recovered — which is why this decoder does not do it, and why every machine-learning method here is judged by whether it predicts measurements it was not shown, rather than by whether it looks better.

Two corrections, kept on the record. An earlier version of this work claimed the stripe alternation was 60 Hz mains hum at ~70% of picture amplitude. Both halves were wrong — measuring the whole record at 25× finer resolution puts it at 60.0436 Hz, which is half the scan rate and not mains. It also claimed the frame geometry was self-contradictory; that was a bad measurement of my own. Full details are in the repository.

What the decoder was allowed to know

This project wants two things that pull against each other: a clean-room decode, recovering the pictures from the artifact alone the way a recipient who has never heard of Earth would have to — and the best pictures we can get, which means using the original slides to check the work. Both are worth having. Mixing them silently would make the result worthless, because you could not tell which claims survive without the answer key. So every shipped artifact carries a tier, and the boundary is asserted in code rather than promised in prose.

TierMay useCovers
0 · Recordthe audio and the engraved cover — what an alien has every decoded pixel, timebase, dot clock, geometry, drift correction, the circle's self-check
1 · Universalpriors any observer could hold without knowing Earth denoising, colour-triplet fusion, deconvolution by the measured lens response
2 · Earththe original slides and their captions — cheating which edge is up, which reproductions are mirrored, every title, red/green/blue roles, all scoring
3 · Oraclesettings chosen per image by looking at the answer not a decoder — a measurement of how much a better honest decoder could still gain

The rule that makes this more than labelling: tier 0 and tier 1 output must be reproducible with no tier 2 file on disk. A check walks the decoder's import graph and fails if a tier 0 module can so much as reach reference material. The decoder imports three modules and reads one file, the WAV.

Rotation is cheating, and always will be. The record never says which edge is the top. We tested whether it at least says which pictures were turned — a slide fed in sideways ought to leave blank bands at the frame edges — and it does not: the 1977 operators reframed each slide to fill the frame, so turning it left no trace in the signal. The detector finds 1 of 66 turned frames. This gallery rotates the pictures anyway, so you needn't tilt your head, but that turn happens when the image is drawn, and is never written into a decoded pixel.

Known contamination, stated rather than hidden. The thumbnails this page loads are currently tier 2 and ought to be tier 0: the build step bakes a hand-made quarter turn into 60 of the 156 files. It is logged as a defect with the fix written down, so the table above cannot be read as a claim of purity that has not been earned.

There is one orientation check the record does provide, and it is the companion to the circle. Four raster conventions fit the cover, and two of them are mirror images of the other two — get it wrong and all 116 pictures are flipped, with nothing in the pictures to catch it. But image 2 is the pulsar map, and the pulsar map is also engraved on the cover, which is bolted to the record. Fourteen lines at fourteen angles is strongly handed, so comparing the decode against the engraving pins the handedness of the whole raster at a stroke. The circle checks your timing; the pulsar map checks your handedness. Neither needs a word of language.

Honest status

This page is a gallery. The decoder itself is Python, it lives in the repository, and you can run it on the master tape in about ten minutes — the README says how, and the first thing it hands you is the calibration circle, which tells you whether your own timebase is right. What you see here was decoded offline. The Paint it from blank button replays a frame's audio and paints the picture in step with it, one trace at a time, exactly as the scan ran; it is faithful, but it is replaying a decode rather than performing one. A browser port is planned and deliberately parked.

The embossed look is fixed. Earlier versions of these images had a bas-relief, hammered-metal quality: nothing in the 1977 chain passed a constant brightness level, so flat areas decayed toward mid-grey and only edges survived. It is the defect the 2017 decoder's author documented and could not solve. It is solved here, by inverting the chain's one-pole high-pass in raster order with a time constant measured per channel from a scene-free part of the record's own signal — 530 samples on the left channel, 295 on the right. The inverse runs along each trace and straight across every trace boundary, because that is how time ran in the recording; a per-trace version of the same filter is a bug, and it shipped here once before it was caught.

Reference images are for checking and labelling only. Nothing on the reference side has ever fed pixel recovery — the decoder's only input is the WAV. Where a book page has not been confidently matched to one of our images, the panel says match uncertain rather than showing a guess, and some images have no reference held at all.

Fourteen published reproductions are printed backwards. Aligning each reference to our decode needs a transform that can mirror, and once it could, fourteen plates snapped into place that had never matched before — the Thai elephant went from 3 to 799 matching features, the Cameroon house from 3 to 503. Bali, Guatemala, Japan, Greece, Turkey, Australia and the whistling swans are all laterally inverted in print. Our decodes had them right. Since the record is the authority on what it contains, it is the reference that has been flipped to match, and the calibration circle — which "wins" a mirror test by one feature on noise alone, being symmetric — was deliberately left alone.

Colour separation order: settled, and the toggle is gone. Published decoders disagreed on whether the three scans run red-green-blue or the reverse. It is answerable by eye: under B G R the Earth's oceans turn brown and the Sinai desert blue-grey; under R G B the oceans are blue, the desert tan and the Red Sea reads correctly. R G B is right, confirming Ron Barry's 2017 table.

But notice what settled it: knowing what colour an ocean is — Earth knowledge, tier 2. The record carries its own key and it is a lovely idea: photograph the solar spectrum, and any chemist anywhere recognises hydrogen's absorption lines and can attach wavelengths to positions. We measured how well it works. It gives the ordering — our three separations peak at 84.2%, 60.4% and 39.3% across the frame, cleanly monotonic, so a recipient learns the filters run in wavelength sequence. It does not give the direction: after removing the smooth envelope the residual line structure is 0.6–1.9 grey levels against an envelope of 60–124, half a percent to 1.6%. The Fraunhofer lines are not readable. So on the record's own evidence R G B versus B G R is a coin flip — which is precisely why humans disagreed about it. A key that its own designers' species cannot read is not yet a key.
